01 Order completion estimate

A completion date that stays honest when suppliers, approvals and the calendar change.

The problem
A service order is made of several jobs. Each job depends on supplier lead times and workshop time, starts only after the customer approves it, and some jobs run in parallel. Customers, operations and business partners all need one reliable completion date, and it has to update whenever the order changes.
What I built
  • A per-job estimate that combines supplier lead time and workshop time and starts from the approval.
  • A working-day calendar: non-working days are skipped and compensated until the result is stable, and approval timing decides when work can start.
  • An order-level estimate where parallel work is counted once instead of being added up.
  • Recalculation on every relevant change, event-driven sync to partner systems through queues, and a backfill for historical orders.
  • The rules isolated in pure, unit-tested domain modules with no framework or database code.
Why it matters
Customers, operations and partners see the same date, and it stays correct as the order changes.

  • MySQL
  • BullMQ
  • Domain modelling
  • Jest

02 Loyalty points

Loyalty points that behave like money, wherever they are earned or spent.

The problem
Customers earn points on purchases and subscriptions, spend them at checkout, and can bring in points from partner programs. Points and currency must convert the same way everywhere, expire on schedule, survive refunds, and never be credited twice.
What I built
  • Configurable earning rules per product and subscription, exposed consistently across the APIs.
  • One rounding policy for points and currency, applied at checkout, on invoices and in balances.
  • Refund handling that restores spent points and reverses earned ones, so a refunded order nets to zero.
  • Scheduled expiry, and notifications sent only after the transaction commits.
  • An idempotent partner transfer API: a retried request returns the original result instead of crediting twice.
Why it matters
Balances stay consistent across checkout, subscriptions and partners, and integrations are safe to retry.
  • Monetary precision
  • Idempotency
  • Scheduled jobs
  • Transactions
  • Partner API

    03 Feature flag platform

    Gradual rollouts where any doubt keeps the feature off.

    The problem
    New features should reach users gradually. If the flag service is slow, not yet synced or failing, nothing may switch on by accident, and business code should not be tied to one vendor.
    What I built
    • A ports-and-adapters design: a provider-agnostic port, an Unleash adapter and a service. Nothing else in the codebase imports the vendor SDK.
    • A custom NestJS decorator and guard: one line gates a route or a whole controller.
    • Fail-closed everywhere: a disabled flag, a sync timeout or any unexpected error inside the guard all deny the same way.
    • Denials go through the app's own exception handling, so users get a clean error instead of an uncaught server error.
    • Unit tests for the adapter and the guard.
    Why it matters
    Features roll out safely, and swapping the flag vendor touches one adapter, not the business code.
    • Unleash
    • Ports and adapters
    • Decorators
    • Guards
    • Jest

    04 Handover verification

    Verifiable handovers, controlled by layered settings.

    The problem
    During a service, a customer's car changes hands more than once. Each handover should be verifiable with a short code, but only where the business has enabled it, and the rules differ for each channel the platform serves.
    What I built
    • A layered settings model, from the broadest level to the most specific, where every layer must allow verification. Admin APIs and validation sit on top.
    • A clear policy for when the answer is decided. I first stored it on each order at creation. When the business wanted setting changes to reach open orders, I moved to a live check at completion and retired the stored value safely.
    • Rules that skip a code when there is nobody on the other side to confirm it, so orders are never blocked for no reason.
    • The same rules enforced across the customer apps, the partner API, B2B integrations and links used by external providers.
    Why it matters
    Handovers can be proven where it matters, and nothing gets stuck where it does not apply.
    • Policy design
    • Layered settings
    • Snapshot vs live
    • Jest
